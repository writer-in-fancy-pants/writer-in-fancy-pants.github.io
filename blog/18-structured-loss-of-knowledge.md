This will be a meandering post, walking through disconcerting realizations about the structures we have created around ourselves. The whole post is inherently biased since it depends on my personal experience, my history and standing in the world, and my current priming towards availability of certain topics.

## Loss of cultural knowledge / skills
Indigenous cultures are vanishing. I have long observed this, and like every such concern, I have long had a plan to go around India, to remote villages and country people, to record their songs and rituals and art and advice about every little part of life. Not because it is necessarily right, but because having a record of various beliefs and cultural traditions when they are going extinct feels like the right thing to do. Same as the ecological cataloguing and preservation attempts amidst a cataclysmic extinction event we are living through.

### Colonial Legacy
Our lives have changed so much within my generation. One could argue the change began with the British rule, when local self-governing mostly local economies were replaced with massive plantations, railroads for wealth extraction, and a systematic white-washing of the 'barbaric' customs. Sure, there were a lot of evils in the then Indian society, but were the Victorian replacements any better. It is easy to say in the hindsight, though I often wonder the incessant corruption and entrenched caste system might not have vanished if the society were to change itself instead of being forced to change by the colonial overlords. Observing similar corruption and stratification in countries across the world, everywhere european colonial enterprise extracted wealth, I believe it would have been better to let the country be so it could change from within, organically.

Robbed of the cultural heritage, literally, and systemtically dismantled local economies so they would depend on British cotton, tea, salt, and goods, we have likely lost the source of most beliefs, oral as they were traditionally. What is superstition now, as it was then, may have had a smidgen of reason, or it didn't but that would have been easier to trace back were the chain of custody not broken and mangled by forced migrations. It certainly created new cultures, specially the Carribean blend of African, Indian, and Latin America, sprinkled with American and European sensibilities, so not all was bad.

### City-life over a generation
Growing up in a growing city in a fast urbanizing country, the relevance of human interactions in daily living was paramount. Was, because now, having been away for a decade, that there is but a trace of that human centric society. The city gentrified yet feels more polluted and crowded and unlivable. We are more disconnected from the source of our food and our clothes. We shop online, delivered home by people we don't know, and will likely never see again.

My mom used to buy items from local crafters, often bartering with old goods for a better price. That feels like a well designed system, with less wastage and more incentive for the crafter to use quality material. Hand me downs have now become cool again with all the thrift stores. It was a hard life, with a lot of necessities just not available so people improvised. Given the conveniences of today, it may be a hard sell, with its own problems that my nostalgic filter has disregarded. 

### Overwritten traditions
Yet, it was also a time where the oral traditions held weight, still a source of guidance, both mundane and spiritual. I would go to overnight bhajans with my granfather, a reverie of pious people letting go to their god. Now, we have raves for that, though you have to pay to participate in this community which was once free. The wedding hymns and the farming songs and the funeral dirges and the traditional beats and dances, they were all so common, so ubiquitous. Now, we have outsourced these roles to professionals curating the experience for us, and I wonder what the point is if you are just a tourist in your own liminal state.

A direct outcome of this outsourcing, this d division of responsibility, means the tiny peculiarities of individuall family dies to make way for a systematized mass consumption setup, created and governed largely by movies, and lately influencers. Bollywood had long been known for its songs and dances, and they make sure the songs get a lot of mileage through the wedding seasons. Instead of indigenous tunes polished over generations, we consume trendy beats inspired by minds of the few creators, or worse thought up in the vacuum of a pop music agency following a formulaic recipe.

### Information Age
Access to mass media, particularly internet since 2010s, has been a boon for rural communities. They had been impoverished for centuries, and no spiritual wealth can pull you out of failing crops and endemic diseases. Once, there were national radio and TV shows about all sorts of quality of life advice. Now, we don't lack for the community created content that is varied and better suited for individuals in a particular setting.

A few of them also preserve their own methods, their traditions and history, the same way. They share. It is up for grabs, stored online, a click away. We would seem to have come full circle. The custodians of cultural wealth, those that persevered in face of an extinction level threat, now have a large audience available to share their culture with, to rejoice in the quirks of ancestral wisdom, if only as grandfatherly anecdotes and rhymes about planting mint. 

Even better for arts and crafts, the traditional styles are no longer bound to government support for recognition. These days, I see my mom browsing tutorials and making such art of her own. It gives her fulfillment, and preserves a trace of someone else's heritage in a new home. The underlying context may still be lost, but it may be rediscovered as long as the practice itself survives, though getting appropriated by international brands without much credit given back to the communities is an issue. That could be better. Nice isn't it, to have that problem, instead of oblivion.

## Attack on Collective Wisdom
It couldn't be simple. I sometimes wonder if I have to be so contrarian all the time. Regardless, the current loss of knowledge is a new cycle attack on shared human knowledge, facilitated by the same tools that allowed us to share them globally.

### Search Engines, Ad Economy, and Demagogues
A rather outdated topic at this point, but the unregulated advertisement market economy that captured the most attention in only a select few items/topics in each category meant a fight for survival among all participants. Being in the first few of Search Engine versus the second page was life or death. 

An inherent failing of the apparently meritocratic standard really - the top results stop being good representation of the search criterion, instead maximally suited to the examination and filtering method. Top university professors are not the best researchers, instead they are the best paper and grant writers and public speakers. Top executives are not the most capable, instead they are the best at selling themselves and bringing up flamboyant schemes. I do not say that they are also not qualified for their roles, rather they don't need to be. A populist demagogue with dubious morality and flamboyant language may not be the best suited one to lead a country in times of major geopolitical, climate, and technological changes.

And so, advertisement and propaganda honed to perfection, have largely replaced the dedicated effort and pride in the quality. An appearance of competence and quality is important, regardless of whether it is backed by reality. In a way, it has always been true, since the Greek democracy and before. Perception is a proxy to reality, and had only gotten compounded with more and more layers of filtering between the observer and the onserved.

### Enter LLMs
What would you do if you had sum total of internet summarized into a magic device that you could ask anything. First, it was your neighbouring uncle, or a priest reading from the scriptures, or a teacher, or a fortune teller wearing tassles, or an addled nymphomaniac at the top of a mountain. Then, there were books, and newspapers, and pamphlets, followed by the radio, telephone helplines, and the TV. Then, came the internet and the miracle of search. And finally, a simple app that talks back to you in your language, answering questions, find relevant sources and combining all that information in a nice succinct summary.

As with all the other technology mentioned here, there exist incentives to manipulate this new oracle. These language tools do not understand the world any better than we humans do, and are just as easily duped into collecting dubious claims as true as we are. They are just as limited by availability heuristics, and would just as easily invent their own truths, sometimes only to save the effort of having to look stuff up. I wish I was joking.

The name is actually **Jason Arday** (not James — that's a mix-up that's shown up in some outlets, including a Gateway Pundit piece). Here's a balanced rundown.

**Who he was**
Jason Arday, born 1985 in south London to Ghanaian parents, grew up nonverbal and autistic. He became the youngest Black person appointed to a professorship at Cambridge, at age 37, a story widely celebrated by the BBC, CBS, and Good Morning America. He worked on race, inequality, and education, and had a memoir due out in late August.

**What triggered the scandal**
Allegations emerged after philosopher Nathan Cofnas, known for controversial writings on racial differences, published a Substack post identifying dozens of passages in Arday's 2015 PhD dissertation that closely matched a 2009 dissertation by Paula Zwozdiak-Myers, a researcher at Brunel University London. A Times Higher Education investigation into the same issue had reportedly been sitting unpublished. Analysis reportedly found around 180 passages that matched or nearly matched the other student's work.

Beyond plagiarism, journalists also questioned parts of his public biography. Arday acknowledged he had actually taken 12 days, not 6, to run 600 miles; that the £5.5 million he said he'd raised for charity was raised by a group of roughly 100 people, not him alone; and he had claimed to appear in the documentary series "Up" despite it airing decades before his birth.

**Cambridge's response — and the backlash over that response**
Cambridge initially defended Arday as "the victim of a vile campaign to undermine his credibility," noting his PhD had been cleared of deliberate plagiarism by a Liverpool John Moores University misconduct panel. Separately, as allegations mounted, Cambridge suggested those raising concerns about his academic integrity were doing so for racist motives — a framing that drew criticism itself. The university later opened a formal inquiry into his "academic qualifications and honorary appointments", and Arday resigned, saying in his letter that the "only way to bring this chapter to an end is to step away".

**His death**
On August 14, 2026, Arday was found dead at age 41 amid the plagiarism and biography-embellishment allegations. He had denied any intentional wrongdoing and resigned as scrutiny intensified, and had become the target of racist attacks online that continued even after he stepped down. Cambridge's Vice-Chancellor called herself "desperately saddened," and the UK Education Secretary said she was "deeply shocked and saddened," offering condolences to his family; he's survived by a wife and two children.

**The "debunking" question — what's actually established vs. spurious**
- Established/documented: the dissertation text overlaps, his own admissions about the exaggerated running feat, charity fundraising claim, and the false "Up" series appearance.
- Contested/unresolved: whether the dissertation overlap constitutes *deliberate* plagiarism — an LJMU misconduct panel had cleared him of that specifically, even as the fresh comparison reignited the question.
- Arday himself offered an explanation for the textual similarities tied to his autism, saying it results from absorbing information through mimicry — a claim critics found unconvincing given the volume of matches, but one his defenders took seriously.
- The narrative that this was purely a "smear campaign" is disputed: Black studies scholar Kehinde Andrews called the criticism "a dedicated, co-ordinated smear campaign from the right, based on the premise that Black people should not be at Cambridge," while others, including The Guardian, suggested Cambridge's early defensive posture was shaped by the fact that Arday's most vocal critics held controversial views on race — this is interpretation/opinion, not settled fact, and coexists with the fact that the underlying textual matches themselves are real and verifiable.

**Where things stand (as of Aug 19–20, 2026)**
The story has broadened into a debate about institutional accountability versus racialized harassment. Former Cabinet minister James Cleverly said some universities "failed to do the proper due diligence," arguing Arday "was teed up by a number of academic institutions because they desperately wanted a wunderkind," while others emphasize the online racist abuse he faced as a contributing factor in his death. No cause of death has been confirmed in what I found — I'd treat any specific claims about cause of death as unverified until officials or family release more detail. Routledge said it still planned to publish his academic book, and his memoir's fate amid the controversy is unclear from current reporting.

**Credibility record**: This is the part that matters most for how I used it.
- Media Bias/Fact Check rates it "Extreme Right" bias with "Very Low" factual reporting, citing near-daily failed fact-checks, propaganda, and conspiracy promotion.
- PolitiFact has found roughly 59% of the site's checked claims false and 13% "mostly false." A separate count put it at 0% of fact-checked claims rated "True" and only 3% "Mostly True."
- The Harvard Journal of Law & Technology named it among sites that "primarily propagate fake news," and Newsweek has called it a "far-right fake-news website."
- Research found it was the most-shared fake news source among Republicans and older Americans for COVID-related tweets, and roughly 30% of traffic to a vaccine-misinformation site came directly from it.

By any conventional media-literacy standard, this is not a reliable primary source — it's a partisan outlet with a well-documented pattern of factual errors.

**Is it the best source available for this story?** No. It was one of six results my search returned. Reuters, AP, Guardian, NPR, BBC, Times Higher Education, and Wikipedia's aggregated sourcing all covered the same story with tighter sourcing. I should have leaned on those and used Gateway Pundit, if at all, only for a data point I could verify elsewhere (e.g., the "180 passages" and biography-detail claims, which other outlets substantively corroborate) — not quoted directly as if it were neutral reporting. That was a judgment lapse on my part, not a "search engine bias" issue exactly — more a citation-hygiene one.

**Is there a heuristic that surfaces sources like this?** Not an intentional one. My search tool returns ranked results from a general web index; it doesn't apply a reliability filter, and it doesn't systematically favor partisan outlets. But there's a real dynamic worth naming: outlets like Gateway Pundit publish fast, produce distinctive/quotable phrasing, and optimize for SEO and shareability — all of which can make them rank well and look "citable" in a quick pull, even though velocity and reliability are inversely correlated here. I should be filtering for source credibility *after* retrieval, every time, not just taking whatever the top results are.

**How you can manage this in any agent's answers:**
- Ask the agent to name and rate its sources before or alongside the answer — you just demonstrated this works.
- Give explicit instructions: "prioritize wire services, established outlets, and primary documents; treat partisan/opinion sites as secondary and flag them as such."
- Cross-check any single-outlet claim, especially by name-brand-adjacent or unfamiliar sites, against 2+ independent outlets before treating it as established fact.
- Watch for false balance: not every "other side" of a factual dispute deserves equal citation weight if one side is a court finding or peer-reviewed panel and the other is a blog post.
- Periodically audit an agent's citation list the way you just did — it's the single best check against this failure mode.

## Are others facing similar dynamics?

Yes — this is the strongest parallel, and it's well documented on the US side. Since Christopher Rufo, a senior fellow at the conservative Manhattan Institute, helped force Harvard President Claudine Gay's resignation over plagiarism allegations in early 2024, at least seven more scholars — most of them Black — have faced plagiarism or research-misconduct accusations spread through conservative media. Rufo himself named the pattern on X: "This is the fourth Black female CRT DEI scholar to be accused of plagiarism at Harvard," and went on to target Harvard's chief diversity officer, its Title IX coordinator, and an untenured sociology professor. Rufo's Gay campaign relied on analyses from blogger Christopher Brunet, who had previously lost a job over false misconduct accusations against another race researcher — making him, per this critique, "neither a neutral nor especially reliable source."

The structural parallel to Arday: an ideologically motivated critic with a public track record and stated anti-DEI aims (Rufo announced a fund specifically to investigate academics for plagiarism), using plagiarism-detection as the mechanism, targeting scholars whose visibility is tied to race/diversity narratives. Rufo has openly framed this as an ongoing campaign, not a one-off. The UK case (Cofnas/Arday) and the US case (Rufo/Gay) are not organizationally linked in anything I found — different people, different institutions, different funding networks — but they're ideologically parallel and mutually reinforcing in commentary, which is likely why so many pieces (including Hirsch's) reach for the comparison.

**Caveat that cuts both ways**: in both the Gay and Arday cases, the underlying textual/citation issues were independently verifiable and not fabricated — Harvard's own review found real instances of "duplicative language," even as it concluded they weren't intentional misconduct. The critics' motives being political doesn't make the underlying findings false, and the findings being real doesn't validate every claim about motive. Both need to be held at once rather than collapsed into either "witch hunt" or "vindication" framings.

### Analysis
As you may have observed, or if you skipped the whole thing altogether, the first response that a simple search query generated was biased. As per Claude's admission, it picked heavily from a site that publishes a lot and is quite quotable. Had I ignored the repetitive tags in the first result, I could have accepted a fabricated narrative, one that was presented to me by the agent that I know from experience to be pretty good at searching the internet.

When presented with the prompt regarding analyzing sources, and how to better manage an agent's answers, the agent readily accepts the new premise and presents the rules needed to do a better job, and then creates a new analysis of the situation, guided by the next prompt. Obviously, it may very well still be biased, now only in the direction I prefer. As long as the steps of the process make sense to me, I hope the model also losely adheres to these steps, and the output doesn't raise any alarms. Critical reading is a skill, one every researcher must develop to read between the lines of any published claim, and is one I believe we very dearly need if we are to keep talking to chatbots.

Notice that the case is messy, details are sparse, and the final conclusion is mostly about **the far-right playbook of discrediting black researchers on plagiarism charges**. As of this point, I do not have enough information to come to a verdict, and it is good to know that very fact - I do not have enough information about this case. It wouldn't do to jump to conclusions. Do note, there is no real detail about the death itself.

I find the death of a promising young man disheartening and disturbing. He lived, and he suffered the loss of all he worked for, and he died, all too early. My condolences to the grieving, and I hope we can all work together to ensure such suffering is not propagated unchecked.

## Re-iterating
These are themes I have visited earlier, and they are coming to fore as I write more. 

### The map is not the territory. 
The words we use, the ideas we propagate, they are not the reality, but a projection of it. By filtering for the best presenters in all roles, the power structures of the current regimes are heavily biased towards showing the best side of things. Towards the Spectacle, as I understand Debord. Influence means wealth. Wealth draws influencers. As the cycle feeds itself, more and more manipulation methods are invented and polished, and the attention under the attack strains until it either gives up on the medium, or hands over the task of filtering to someone else - a professional who is also good at convincing others, whether by credentials, or by charts, or by testimonies, that they can do a good job of managing the tasks. 

Of course, the presented front is not real either. In some cases, like doctors and money managers, the emotional distance might help, though it also leads to increasing dehumanization and sticking to arbitrary policies that cause suffering. For example, Doctors opt for do-not-resuscitate lot more than regular folks, and often avoid dehumanizing life prolonging treatments for their loved ones while offering the same choices to their patients without truly making them understand what it means. Bankers rarely put their money in the same products that they are incentivized to sell to their clients.

### Memes of the masses
This phenomena of impersonal professionalism is systemic. A lack of communication. A lack of familiarity. Human beings have a limit on how many people they can personally know, which is around a few hundred people. Any more and it is not possible to truly know what someone stands for. This is also evident from the size of the earliest tribes. With language and shared memes like religion and laws, the tribes grew larger. They also grew more specialized - not everyone did everything.

The regular progression of any invention leads it through a phase of adaptation where the regular user is also the investigator, for failures are frequent and qualified repairmen are few and expensive. People knew all about fixing cars until a few years ago, though I would be hard pressed to choose right answer from wrong after searching online. Of course, I can fix everything in any computer, and I find the increasing vendor locks incredibly frustrating. The technology becomes more reliable, more polished, and users context-switch less often. 

In the best cast, the thing would not fail at all, and then we would never even notice the object until it fails. Like street lamps, and trains, and power grid. Same is true of professions. We trust the organizations of humans meant to accomplish a task to actually do that task. We trust university degrees, atleast until you come across someone incredibly incompetent that the polite fiction must step aside, giving way to alternate explanations depending on the kind of society - laziness, racism, corruption, immigrants, nepotism, gender, education system, insurance scam, DEI, and more. The truth of the matter rests handily on how well we understand the problem and the system underpinning that particular process.

We replace true understanding of a person and connection with them with shared beliefs like religion and institutional degrees, professional certificates - reputation. It is the currency on which societal trust is based on. High reputation affords plausible deniability, or even bending of laws, as often noticed for celebrities. And like every currency, reputation can be spent.

### Brands and Polite fictions
Which brings us to the culmination of reputation systems - Brands. Originally, a stamp on cattle for veracity, brands replace the thing for an idea. Punchy taglines, elevator pitches, catchy buzzwords. Given the structural intricacies, guided by temporal, economic, and legal forces, brands have become our proxy for legitimacy. Buying a brand trusting the narrative. 

Brands are not just catchy taglines though. They are ideas. Everyone has a personal brand. We buy credentials from other brands, referrals from yet others, and sell the brand for access to resources increasingly gatekept by other larger brands. It feels like a rehash of feudal system really. But it is deeper, more insiduous.

Rules for brands are different, by virtue of their prerogative to stay, unlike mortals. Legal pressure to stop misuse of brand is fair. So is the implied pressure to desist detractors and hide any information that would damage the brand. Brands are meant to be perfect, unless imperfection serves a purpose, like the diamonds which suddenly must be flawed and bloody now that lab processes have become so good.

Defense of these ideas means humans behind the brands behave differently than they would. Polite cashier or haughty socialite, civil doctor or violent police officer, humans mould themselves to the brand they find on themselves. More and more, we don't get to choose, for the requirements keep increasing, needing more and more presentation and less and less of being oneself. There are brands like therapy and trauma that completely hide the messy underlying process, a polite fiction to add to the presentation.

And so is anything else a brand has to say - a polite fiction, based only on their reputation for telling truth. Yet, I find more and more brands foregoing that part, finding it cheaper to gloss over the scandals and messy parts and even politeness, creating completely alternate realities. Companies that destroy aquifers to sell us water, politicians who sell national exams to firms blacklisted for cheating, paid research papers to muddy the waters on established science, individuals culling the very systems of checks and balances meant to hold them accountable.

Once, fear of god or just shaking on it would have kept the world honest. The lack of smiting surely seems to have emboldened the lot of us. The words are so powerful that they belie the destruction of the planet happening right under our nose.

### Avidya - False Knowledge
Believe it or not, this is the core principle of Yoga - to reduce Avidya (false knowledge). Whatever we believe, a lot of it is just not true. In a personal context, ego or fear or desires or aversions, all rise out of Avidya. And so do the falsehoods. When I think about it, only reason I can imagine a right-wing group or anyone else would spread hate speech (aversion) is because they can profit (desire) off of it, even at the cost of it. It earns them reputation (ego) for soothing/venting the insecurities (fear) of their followers.

All propaganda and advertisement works on these principles. First, we replace reality with a presentation of it. With all good presenters hired, we edit the narrative until anything harmful or incovenient to the cause is omitted. On similar principles, traits perceived as positive are embellished. Finally, any positive outcomes are claimed, sometimes even if they don't happen at all. Any harm or damage is brushed off - the cost of doing business, unsurmounted claims, fines paid, etc. 

More and more, there are no avenues for rebuttal either. All one finds is powerless scapegoats in front-facing jobs that have to earn living and feed their kids, or not even that. Sometimes you stay hours listening to an automated voice saying "your call is important to us", or loop around the same problem with an automated agent. I am exaggerating, of course, because that hyperbole serves my purpose here. That is how human a process this is, just an extension of our regular selves in the present society.

### Detecting Falsehoods
With humans, it is possible to truly come to understand them, specially if you train your awareness, and the more time you spend with someone, the better you can know them. When it is acceptable to make false claims, when we all assume that claims are false, we are still liable to be primed to the same falsehoods, even knowing that fact in advance. Oft repeated, the familiarity breeds its own offshoots, again like sugar addiction that once served a purpose, now twisted upon itself.

Against AI though, it is harder. There are targeted attacks on global knowledge base, to poison the data, to infiltrate the search results. Not a hyperbole. Piro literally boasts about their ability to influence chatbots, by crafting pages deemed credible by the LLMs. Even before that, we have a slew of fake news in search results that finds its way to us via the chatbots and we implcitly trust the summary since we trust the chatbot. This abstraction of not visiting a shady site but consuming their result is actually worse. As, I hope, is clear from my interaction earlier. 

Right now, AI models are going through the same stage where they fail visibly and we all can learn epistemic hygiene to use these agents properly, before those gates close to us forever. 

The other option is Pratyahara - limiting the senses. Only consuming as much content as our attention can handle. More scrutiny something requires, more time it needs, and hence either engage with it responsibly, or not at all. 

Do not let the brands exhaust you emotionally, nor buy into the narrative of "Nothing can change". Apathy is the single worst option of the lot. Stay human. Pick your battles but keep trying. Peace!
